Leonardo di ser Piero da Vinci  ·  1452 – 1519

Saper Vedere Saper Vedere

Knowing how to see. His phrase for the discipline underneath every art and every science he touched.

He wrote from right to left, in mirror image, across some seven thousand surviving sheets. What follows is a codex in six chapters — his method, his paintings, his folios, three of his machines rebuilt so they turn, two of his paintings opened so you can walk inside, and a mirror of your own. The page you are reading obeys the rules set out in Chapter I.

Capitolo I

Il Metodo

The method — and the rules of this page

Leonardo never wrote the treatise he kept promising. What survives is a method, scattered across the notebooks and recoverable from them. Below, each of his principles is stated, and then stated again as the rule it imposed on the document you are reading. This chapter is the specification for its own presentation.

    Capitolo II

    Le Opere

    Fewer than twenty paintings, and half of them unfinished

    He completed almost nothing. The accepted corpus runs to about fifteen panels depending on who is counting, several abandoned mid-work, and it was still enough to reorganise European painting. Take the lens to any plate. Open the annotations only after you have looked.

      Capitolo III

      I Fogli

      The sheets

      Roughly seven thousand pages survive, perhaps a quarter of what he made. They are not organised. Anatomy shares a sheet with a shopping list; a siege engine shares one with a riddle. Below they are sorted into the five preoccupations he kept returning to, which is a convenience he never granted himself.

      Capitolo IV

      Le Macchine Vive

      The machines, set in motion

      Three devices, rebuilt from the drawings as line models you can turn over in your hands. They are shown as he would have shown them — construction lines rather than surfaces — because in every case the interesting thing is the linkage, and in two of the three cases the interesting thing is also why it could never have worked.

      Capitolo V

      Dentro i Dipinti

      Inside the paintings

      A painting in one-point perspective is a set of instructions for rebuilding a room. Here two of them are carried out. The refectory of the Last Supper is reconstructed from its own orthogonals and made walkable; the Mona Lisa is pulled apart into the depth planes it was assembled from. Both scenes stay asleep until you enter them.

      The refectory

      Santa Maria delle Grazie, Milan, 1495–98. The painted architecture is not decoration: it is a continuation of the real room, calculated so that the wall appears to open. Every orthogonal converges on Christ's right temple.

      Walk forward and the coffers close over you. Find the point where the room stops distorting — that is where Leonardo stood to set it out.

      The depth of a face

      The picture is built in receding planes: the parapet and its lost columns, the sitter, the middle ground of road and bridge, the impossible blue mountains, and the haze between them all. Separated, the construction becomes obvious.

      The haze is the point. He worked out that air is not transparent, and then painted the air.

      Capitolo VI

      Lo Specchio

      The mirror

      He wrote right to left, letters reversed, so that his notebooks read correctly only in a mirror. The romantic explanation is secrecy. The likelier one is that he was left-handed, and writing backwards keeps the hand out of the wet ink. Write something below and watch it go over.
